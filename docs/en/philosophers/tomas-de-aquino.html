Thomas Aquinas (c. 1224/25–1274)

Dominican friar, theologian and philosopher, the leading figure of thirteenth-century scholasticism. His synthesis of Aristotelian philosophy and Christian doctrine, set out above all in the Summa theologiae and the Summa contra gentiles, became a central reference of the Catholic tradition.[1] [2] [3]

Life and works

YearEvent
c. 1224/25 ≈Born at Roccasecca, a castle of a noble family near Aquino, between Rome and Naples (the SEP gives 'around 1225'; Britannica, '1224/25').[1] [3]
1230s ≈Studies as a child at the Benedictine abbey of Monte Cassino, where his family expected him to make his career.[1]
c. 1244/45 ≈As an adolescent student at the University of Naples, decides to join the newly founded Order of Preachers (Dominicans) at about twenty; his family, opposed, keeps him detained at Roccasecca for about a year before relenting.[1] [3]
1245–1248 ≈Studies philosophy in Paris as a Dominican friar; in 1248 he is sent to Cologne, under the direction of Albert the Great.[1]
1252–1256Nominated by Albert, returns to Paris for the advanced degree in theology; composes the commentary on Peter Lombard's Sentences (Scriptum, 1252–56).[1]
1256–1259Master of theology, holds the Dominican chair at the University of Paris.[1]
c. 1259–1265 ≈Back in Italy, teaches in Dominican houses; composes the Summa contra gentiles (c. 1258–64, per Britannica).[1] [3]
1265/66 ≈Begins the Summa theologiae, his greatest work, left unfinished at his death (1265/66–73, per Britannica).[3] [1]
1268–1272Second Paris regency, marked by the controversies over radical Aristotelianism ('Averroism'); writes commentaries on Aristotle's principal works.[1]
1272Returns to Italy, to Naples, where he spends his last teaching years.[1]
7 March 1274Dies at the Cistercian abbey of Fossanova, in Lazio.[1] [3]
