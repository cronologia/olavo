Socrates (469–399 BC)

Athenian philosopher who wrote nothing, known through the testimonies of Plato, Xenophon and Aristophanes. His practice of interrogating fellow citizens about the virtues, and his condemnation to death for impiety, made him a founding figure of Western philosophical ethics.[1] [2] [3]

469–399 BC ≈Works: he wrote nothing; his thought is known above all through Plato's dialogues, Xenophon's Socratic writings and Aristophanes' satire — the disparity between the portraits constitutes the 'Socratic problem'.[1] [2]
spring 399 BCTried for impiety and for corrupting the young, he is condemned and executed by poison (hemlock) at seventy, in Athens.[1] [3]

How he read him

Reception, not philosophy: each entry records how Olavo de Carvalho characterised this philosopher, attributed and dated to the 2002 course. The project does not adjudicate who is right. The recordings are machine-transcribed and unchecked against audio, so everything here is paraphrase and nothing is quoted.

  • The session turns on a distinction between three figures the tradition confuses: the philosopher as martyr, the philosopher as leader and ruler, and the philosopher as his community's teacher and guide but not its chief. Socrates is the first and the third; the second is the error he lays at Plato's door.

    Curso História Essencial da Filosofia, session 02 — the Socratic project — 2002 ?

  • Philosophical authority is real but powerless, and he argues it has to be. A philosopher claiming power equal to his cognitive authority would have to be an absolute tyrant — and that is incompatible with how wisdom is acquired, which needs trial, error and an interior freedom a philosopher-king could not allow.

    Curso História Essencial da Filosofia, session 03 — Socrates and Plato — 2002 ?
