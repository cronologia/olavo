Plato (c. 428/427–348/347 BC)

Athenian philosopher, disciple of Socrates and teacher of Aristotle, founder of the Academy. His dialogues, which put into circulation themes such as the theory of Forms, the immortality of the soul and the just city, are among the founding texts of Western philosophy.[1] [2] [3]

Life and works

YearEvent
c. 428/427 BC ≈Born in Athens to an aristocratic family (the SEP gives '429?'; recent scholarship cited in the SEP's Socrates chronology proposes 424/423 BC).[3] [2] [1]
399 BCDeath of Socrates, to whose circle Plato belonged; after the execution he leaves Athens and spends a period in Megara, followed by travels the tradition attributes to him (Cyrene, Italy, Egypt).[2]
c. 387 BC ≈First journey to Italy and Sicily; in Syracuse he attaches himself to Dion, brother-in-law of the tyrant Dionysius I.[2]
c. 387–380 BC ≈Back in Athens, founds the Academy, the school Britannica places in the 380s BC and describes as a remote ancestor of the modern university.[2] [3]
before c. 387 BC ≈Dialogues of the so-called early (Socratic) period, on the usual stylometric chronology: Apology, Crito, Euthyphro, Laches, Charmides, Gorgias, Protagoras, among others.[2] [1]
c. 380–360 BC ≈Dialogues of the middle period, including the Phaedo, the Symposium and the Republic (books II–X), in which the theory of Forms is articulated.[2] [1]
c. 367 and 361 BC ≈Second and third journeys to Syracuse, involving him with Dion in the attempt to influence the young tyrant Dionysius II — episodes narrated in the Seventh Letter, whose authenticity is disputed.[2]
late period ≈Late dialogues, among them the Theaetetus, Sophist, Statesman, Timaeus, Philebus and the Laws; the corpus transmitted under his name also includes letters and works of disputed authenticity.[2] [1]
348/347 BCDies in Athens at about eighty; according to Diogenes Laertius he was buried at the Academy.[3] [2]

How he read him

Reception, not philosophy: each entry records how Olavo de Carvalho characterised this philosopher, attributed and dated to the 2002 course. The project does not adjudicate who is right. The recordings are machine-transcribed and unchecked against audio, so everything here is paraphrase and nothing is quoted.

  • He says outright that he is not a great admirer of Plato, and calls the philosopher-king a monumental error: beyond knowing the universal law a ruler needs authority, and he asks how Plato failed to notice he had none — where Moses and Muhammad had it and were obeyed for millennia.

    Curso História Essencial da Filosofia, session 02 — the Socratic project — 2002 ?

  • He reads the Sicilian debacle as a caricature of Socrates' fate: Plato tries to steer a coup in a neighbouring state, it fails, the conspirators are killed or imprisoned and Plato is sold as a slave and bought back by a student. Socrates, on his reading, is the philosopher who knows he cannot be king.

    Curso História Essencial da Filosofia, session 03 — Socrates and Plato — 2002 ?

  • On the three worlds: the world of forms is not a superior world above the one man inhabits — it is that world. Man stands wholly in neither temporality nor the sensible, which is why the Plato-Aristotle dispute over whether the forms exist in themselves cannot be settled: on his reading neither the forms nor the sensible world exist in themselves, only within eternity.

    Curso História Essencial da Filosofia, session 03 — Socrates and Plato — 2002 ?
