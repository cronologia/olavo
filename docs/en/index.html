1950s
1954 c. 1947–1954 ? Brazil Childhood illness — his own account — His most repeated origin story in the course: sick from birth and bedridden through his first seven years with constant fever, then suddenly and inexplicably cured around age seven, going straight to school afterward (told, among other places, in the lectures of 9 October 2010 and 20 April 2013). In a lecture of 16 January 2010 he added that he was born with an undiagnosed tracheal tumor still with him in his sixties; in a lecture of c. 2020 he tied his late-diagnosed emphysema to a lung operation decades earlier. All of this is his own retrospective testimony via the community transcriptions, unverified against audio — recorded here as testimony, not as a medical record. A second line of his own testimony describes the condition itself, twice. On 10 August 2009 he said he had been born with a tracheal tumour and an infectious bronchitis that was near-fatal for seven years — though a fortnight later he said instead that nobody had known what he had, a wobble worth recording rather than smoothing. Again on his programme of 14 April 2010 — eight years before the surgery and twelve before his death — he said he had been born with a tracheal tumour, which he sized by comparison to an orange. In the same passage he said he had smoked since fifteen, three packs a day for over forty years. Both are his own statements in machine-transcribed broadcasts and neither is a medical record; the first is the earliest first-person account yet found of the congenital condition that the 2018 surgery would remove, and it long predates the disputes that followed his death.[37] [76]
1960s
1966 1966 ? São Paulo, Brazil Brief affiliation with the Brazilian Communist Party (PCB) — A BBC Brasil profile built on an in-person interview (Fellet, December 2016) states 'Entre 1966 e 1968, militou no Partido Comunista'; the historian Gilberto Calil records a brief PCB passage in the 1960s without exact dates. The dating rests on his own recollection as relayed by the interviewer, so the flag stays. In the course he bracketed the period himself: in 1964 he was a secondary student with vague leftist sympathy, not yet a militant, and he recalled joining the party 'to stir up trouble' and spending twenty years working the influence out of himself (lectures of 2009 and 2014, per the community transcriptions).[56] [29] [41] [37]
1970s
1977 1977-02 São Paulo, Brazil First contributions to Folha de S.Paulo — Both Wikipedias date his first Folha de S.Paulo piece (in the Folhetim section, on The Magic Flute) to February 1977. Other sources push his journalism back earlier — Gazeta do Povo says he started at 17 at Folha da Manhã; Calil says he began writing for major newspapers in 1967. The sources do not reconcile these, and they may describe different roles. His own telling in the course: working from age 14 to ease his mother's burden, and entering journalism at 17 expressly because the legal five-hour day left time to study (lecture of 13 November 2010, among others), with the professional registry later obtained by time of service.[40] [41] [9] [37]
1979 1979 São Paulo, Brazil Co-founds the Escola Júpiter de Astrologia — Worked professionally as an astrologer c. 1979–1982, co-founding the school and its Revista de Astrologia Júpiter and collaborating on a university-extension astrology course at PUC-SP; Calil (2021) reports the school had about 140 students by 1980. He later abandoned and disavowed astrology. His own telling in the course: professionally an astrologer for about two years, with some 5,000 charts interpreted — psychiatrists sent him patients' horoscopes — and the school closing around 1980; he quit after judging the techniques unserious.[41] [40] [29] [9] [37]
1980s
1980 1980 ? São Paulo, Brazil First book: A Imagem do Homem na Astrologia — The 1980 date belongs to a 38-page booklet (Ed. Júpiter), grown out of the 1979 PUC-SP astrology course. Olavo's own CV opens its 'Livros publicados' list with three 1983 titles (Speculum) — which explains Agência Brasil's 'first book, 1983': the two dates describe different things (a course booklet vs. his first books proper). The 1980 edition is attested only by book-trade listings, so the flag stays.[41] [1] [55]
1982 1982 ? Brazil Enters the Traditionalist / Perennialist milieu — Per Calil (2021), around 1982 he joined an esoteric 'Tradition' group. Sedgwick's peer-reviewed history of Brazilian Traditionalism (2021) documents the arc: an interest in Traditionalism from 1977, after he was given Jacob Needleman's anthology The Sword of Gnosis; Michel Veber's 1980 lecture on Guénon at the Escola Júpiter and a further series in 1981; his own translation of Guénon's La Métaphysique orientale (A Metafísica Oriental, Escola Júpiter, 1981); an article in the New Age magazine Planeta (no. 107, August 1981) calling Veber 'my professor' — in which he also wrote that Veber had studied under Guénon's personal guidance, which Sedgwick notes was impossible, Veber having been four years old when Guénon left France in 1930; the founding of an Instituto de Estudos Tradicionais; and the first Traditionalist article to appear in a major Brazilian newspaper, 'Moralidade sem Deus?' (Jornal da Tarde, 27 February 1982). He also led followers into A Tradição, the organization of Omar Ali Shah, and broke with it after concluding its leader was a 'con-man' (Sedgwick, quoting his term) — a public dispute that reached the police. Approximate dating for the group entry itself; all characterizations are these scholars'.[29] [30] [75]
1984 1984 ? Brazil Involvement with Sufi Islam (the Maryamiyya of Frithjof Schuon) — Calil (2021) states he converted to Islam in 1984, took the name Sidi Mohammad Ibrahim, and set up a tariqa of his own that dissolved within months. Sedgwick (2021) documents the sequence from the Lings correspondence: after contacting Marco Pallis, Carvalho and some of his followers corresponded with Martin Lings, who explained that 'entering Islam' was the appropriate preparation for the Maryami tariqa — which they did. Lings's letters address him as 'Sidi Muhammad' from June 1985; they were deposited at the São Paulo state court in 1990 as evidence in a case brought by a former follower, which Carvalho won. Sedgwick places him at the 1985 Traditionalist conference in Lima where Lings was present, but marks that presence as deduced rather than documented; he records that in 1986 Carvalho led a group of Brazilians to Bloomington, Indiana, where Schuon appointed him muqaddam (Sufi representative) for Brazil. Carvalho himself later said he had 'entered' Islam rather than converted, in Guénon's sense. On the exit Sedgwick is only relative — 'within a few years' he became disenchanted, telling an interviewer he had discovered that Schuon backed a rival for the leadership of the Brazilian Maryamiyya and had endorsed rumours against him — which converges with his own telling in the course: met Schuon in 1986, made muqaddam on arrival, stayed about a year and a half, provoked his own expulsion, placing the exit around 1987–88 (per the community transcriptions). He later disavowed the period's books as reflecting 'more a duty of obedience' to Schuon than his own thought.[29] [30] [75] [37]
1986 1986 São Paulo, Brazil Publishes Fronteiras da Tradição, his first book beyond astrology — Lectures from his Traditionalist teaching period — several first given at the Editora Astrociência in Rio de Janeiro — are published as Fronteiras da Tradição (São Paulo: Nova Stella, 1986), per Sedgwick (2021) his first book on a topic other than astrology. Its references are 'almost without exception' the major Traditionalist authors; it urges an orthodox religion as the exoteric frame for esoteric practice and warns against pseudo-initiatic organizations, citing A Tradição as an example. The volume also reprints the 1982 Jornal da Tarde article.[75]

São Paulo, Brazil (taught from the United States) Teaching Brazil by videoconference from the United States — His own telling on the True Outspeak broadcasts of April 2007: the Seminário de Filosofia he had been running in Brazil since at least 2002 was by then reaching São Paulo by videoconference from the United States, hosted at É Realizações, and he announced a session for the 18th of that month. 2009 2009-03-14 Online (from Virginia, USA) First lecture of the Curso Online de Filosofia (COF) — The paid weekly online philosophy course he taught until his death — 585 lectures over thirteen years. A student index and English Wikipedia date the first class to 14 March 2009. The community transcription corpus of the course is public (see references); this project cites it by lecture number and date only. His own broadcasts bracket the launch closely: on 9 March 2009 he announced the first class for 'next Saturday, the 14th', and a week later reported that they had expected about a hundred people and more than four hundred arrived, collapsing the streaming provider and forcing the bandwidth to be widened. The start had slipped for five months — promised 'next month' in December 2008, then 'February, March at the latest' in January, then 'a week, ten days at most' in late February — and the delivered format was a members-only stream with an open Brazilian phone line rather than the Skype arrangement he had described in October 2008. It began fortnightly and was weekly by that August.[36] [40] [37] [76]

2010 2010-10-30 Online (from Virginia, USA) Names Padre Paulo Ricardo as his personal confessor — In the lecture of 30 October 2010 he described Padre Paulo Ricardo de Azevedo as his personal confessor; a year earlier (28 November 2009) he had called him the only priest in Brazil he trusted, and in May 2010 he recounted the priest's recent visit to his Virginia home. Sedgwick (2021) reads it as a debate about global politics rather than doctrine: Dugin began it believing he was debating a fellow Brazilian Traditionalist and ended disappointed, while Carvalho showed no interest in common ground — 'the debate, then, was not really about Traditionalism at all'.[33] [11] [75]

2022 2022-01-24 Richmond area, Virginia, USA Dies in hospital, aged 74 — The family's statement, posted to his social accounts in the early hours of 25 January, said he died 'na noite de 24 de janeiro' and gave no cause; no official cause of death was ever released. The cause was publicly disputed: his daughter Heloísa — publicly estranged from her father for years — said he 'died of Covid'; his personal physician, Ahmed Youssif El Tassa, stated the death resulted from acute respiratory failure caused by pulmonary emphysema, a chronic condition, associated with congestive heart failure, bacterial pneumonia from methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and generalized infection, and was 'not related to COVID'; critical outlets noted the COVID diagnosis announced days earlier and his public minimization of the pandemic. His own lectures had recounted a lifelong pulmonary history — bedridden through his first seven years and, in a c. 2020 lecture, decades of asymptomatic emphysema after an old lung operation (per the community transcriptions). The philosophers he referenced

The twenty philosophers most engaged by the Curso Online de Filosofia, by measured presence in the 585-lecture corpus. Each page carries the thinker's own life and works, and the record of which lectures engage him.

Aristóteles (384–322 BC)

Greek philosopher, disciple of Plato and tutor of Alexander of Macedon, founder of the Lyceum in Athens. His surviving treatises cover logic, physics, biology, metaphysics, ethics, politics, rhetoric and poetics, and shaped much of ancient, medieval and modern philosophy.

COF reception: present in 422 files; 189 lectures engage him.

Descartes (1596–1650)

French philosopher, mathematician and scientist, often described as the founder of modern philosophy. He created analytic geometry, formulated the first modern version of mind–body dualism, and proposed a method of systematic doubt as the foundation of knowledge.

COF reception: present in 261 files; 102 lectures engage him.

Platão (c. 428/427–348/347 BC)

Athenian philosopher, disciple of Socrates and teacher of Aristotle, founder of the Academy. His dialogues, which put into circulation themes such as the theory of Forms, the immortality of the soul and the just city, are among the founding texts of Western philosophy.

COF reception: present in 315 files; 114 lectures engage him.

Marx (1818–1883)

German philosopher, economist and revolutionary, author (with Friedrich Engels) of the Communist Manifesto and of Capital. His theory of history, his critique of political economy and the political movement he inspired profoundly marked the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

COF reception: present in 322 files; 105 lectures engage him.

Tomás de Aquino (c. 1224/25–1274)

Dominican friar, theologian and philosopher, the leading figure of thirteenth-century scholasticism. His synthesis of Aristotelian philosophy and Christian doctrine, set out above all in the Summa theologiae and the Summa contra gentiles, became a central reference of the Catholic tradition.

COF reception: present in 242 files; 82 lectures engage him.

Kant (1724–1804)

German philosopher, a central figure of modern philosophy, who spent his whole life in Königsberg. His 'critical philosophy', set out in the three Critiques, proposed that human reason legislates both for the knowledge of nature and for morality.

COF reception: present in 222 files; 75 lectures engage him.

Sócrates (469–399 BC)

Athenian philosopher who wrote nothing, known through the testimonies of Plato, Xenophon and Aristophanes. His practice of interrogating fellow citizens about the virtues, and his condemnation to death for impiety, made him a founding figure of Western philosophical ethics.

COF reception: present in 182 files; 57 lectures engage him.

René Guénon (1886–1951)

French metaphysician and writer, the principal formulator of the twentieth-century 'Traditionalist' or 'Perennialist' current. He wrote on Eastern metaphysics, symbolism, initiation and the critique of the modern world; he embraced Islam and lived his last twenty years in Cairo.

COF reception: present in 118 files; 32 lectures engage him.

Hegel (1770–1831)

German philosopher, the leading exponent of post-Kantian German idealism. He elaborated a system spanning logic, philosophy of nature and philosophy of spirit, with lasting influence on philosophy, political theory and the historiography of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

COF reception: present in 168 files; 32 lectures engage him.

Louis Lavelle (1883–1951)

French philosopher, one of the principal French metaphysicians of the twentieth century and a representative of the 'philosophie de l'esprit' current. Professor at the Sorbonne and the Collège de France, he developed an ontology of participation in being, articulated in the tetralogy La dialectique de l'éternel présent.

COF reception: present in 93 files; 30 lectures engage him.

Nietzsche (1844–1900)

German philosopher and cultural critic, trained as a classical philologist, who published intensely through the 1870s and 1880s. He is known for his radical critiques of traditional European morality, religion and philosophy, and for concepts such as the will to power, the eternal recurrence and the Übermensch. His productive career ended abruptly with the mental collapse of 1889.

COF reception: present in 156 files; 39 lectures engage him.

Leibniz (1646–1716)

German polymath — philosopher, mathematician, jurist and diplomat — counted among the great rationalists of the seventeenth century. He developed the differential and integral calculus independently of Newton and formulated a metaphysical system centered on the monads and pre-established harmony. He spent most of his career in the service of the House of Hanover.

COF reception: present in 144 files; 17 lectures engage him.

Mário Ferreira dos Santos (1907–1968)

Brazilian philosopher, translator and writer, creator of the system he named 'concrete philosophy'. In little more than fifteen years he published the monumental Enciclopédia de Ciências Filosóficas e Sociais, in dozens of volumes, working outside the university establishment. He fell into relative obscurity after his death and has been republished since the 2000s, with the rights to his work acquired by the publisher É Realizações.

COF reception: present in 141 files; 20 lectures engage him.

Santo Agostinho (354–430)

Bishop of Hippo and one of the most influential Christian thinkers of all time, the bridge between ancient philosophy and medieval thought. A professor of rhetoric converted to ascetic Christianity in 386, he left more than five million words of surviving writings, among them the Confessions and The City of God.

COF reception: present in 136 files; 19 lectures engage him.

Eric Voegelin (1901–1985)

German-American political philosopher, formed in Vienna, who fled Nazism in 1938 and made his career in the United States and Munich. He sought a comprehensive philosophy of man, society and history, and became known for his analysis of modern political movements as 'gnosticism'. His principal works include The New Science of Politics and the Order and History series.

COF reception: present in 132 files; 26 lectures engage him.

Husserl (1859–1938)

A mathematician by training and the founder of phenomenology, the movement that marked all of twentieth-century continental philosophy. He made his career at Halle, Göttingen and Freiburg, where Heidegger succeeded him. Of Jewish origin, he spent his last years excluded from German university life under the Nazi regime.

COF reception: present in 107 files; 24 lectures engage him.

Heidegger (1889–1976)

A central figure of twentieth-century European philosophy, author of Being and Time (1927) and renewer of the question of the meaning of being. Husserl's disciple and successor at Freiburg, he influenced existentialism, hermeneutics and deconstruction. His involvement with Nazism — the 1933–34 rectorate and party membership until 1945 — remains an object of controversy.

COF reception: present in 99 files; 28 lectures engage him.

Frithjof Schuon (1907–1998)

Swiss-German writer and metaphysician, the leading exponent of the perennialist (Traditionalist) school in the twentieth century alongside René Guénon and Ananda Coomaraswamy. Initiated into Sufism in Algeria in 1933, he directed from Europe — and, after 1980, from Bloomington, Indiana — the order that became known as the Maryamiyya, and wrote dozens of books on metaphysics, comparative religion and sacred art. The accounts of his life here follow chiefly his estate's site and the historian of Traditionalism Mark Sedgwick.

Ortega y Gasset (1883–1955)

Spanish philosopher, essayist and intellectual leader, formed in German neo-Kantianism and creator of 'vital reason' ('I am I and my circumstance'). Professor of metaphysics in Madrid and founder of the Revista de Occidente, he became world-famous with The Revolt of the Masses. He left Spain with the Civil War and returned after the Second World War.

Dante Alighieri (c. 1265–1321)

Poet, political thinker and 'father of the Italian language', author of the Divine Comedy, the poetic synthesis of medieval culture. He took an active part in the civic life of Florence until his condemnation to exile in 1302, and wrote in banishment his great prose works and the poem that immortalized him. He died in Ravenna in 1321.

The selves, and the immortality of the soul Os eus e a imortalidade da alma

A distinction between the self that senses and acts now, the self others know, the self assembled from remembered life, and a permanent substantial self underneath — which he identifies flatly with the immortal soul, calling the other three merely its functions. Knowledge by presence Conhecimento por presença

Beneath perception, memory and theory there is a prior, non-representational cognition that consists in reality simply being present to the knower. He calls it the most exact form of knowledge precisely because it cannot be turned into a thought one possesses. Its work is to explain how anyone ever corrects a framework from inside it: without it, he argues, a powerful philosophy would hypnotise its adherents completely and no school could revise itself.

Lineage: He names Suhrawardī and the Persian's Philosophy of Illumination — but says he had grasped the thing before reading him, that Suhrawardī used the phrase not quite in his sense, and the attribution appears only seven months after he begins using the term. Cognitive parallax Paralaxe cognitiva

A displacement between the axis of a thinker's lived experience and the axis of his theoretical construction — seeing one thing while theorising another. He claims the phenomenon as his own discovery and insists it is a cognitive defect rather than hypocrisy or lying.

Where it comes from: The worked case is older than the term's exposition in the course: he points out that he had already given it in O Jardim das Aflições (1995) — Marx announcing a materialist philosophy and delivering one whose real substance is human action, with nature entering only as raw material. He generalises from it that a philosophy built on a parallax is not so much false as unrealisable, so its author ends up doing a third thing neither he nor his readers can name.

He revised it: He narrowed it by 2013 to strict performative self-refutation — where the act of saying something contradicts what is said — explicitly excluding mere self-contradiction, lying and propaganda, and presenting the narrowing as a correction of how his coinage was being used.

He credits: Marx Descartes Kant

The theory of the four discourses A teoria dos quatro discursos

His most widely deployed framework: four kinds of discourse — poetic, rhetorical, dialectical and analytical — graded by the certainty they can carry, which he treats as one scale rather than four separate disciplines. The order is a dependency, not a preference: he calls it the natural sequence of a human education, in which one learns to imagine and express the world before arguing about conduct, and argues about conduct before demonstrating anything. He read the Aristotelian corpus in that order and held that Aristotle's logic becomes unintelligible once it is amputated from his rhetoric.

He credits: Aristotle Aquinas

The theory of Empire A teoria do Império

Empire is the recurring idea of Western history, and he traces its transmutations from the fall of Rome to the American expansion — each collapse followed by someone assembling the next one. His definition is the load-bearing part: an empire is a supranational organisation that does not federate nations but gathers forces from inside them, which is why it can operate against the interests of the very country that hosts it.

Where it comes from: Set out in O Jardim das Aflições (1995), of which he says a great part is a history of the transmutations of the idea of empire — a great part, not the whole: the book is subtitled an essay on materialism and civil religion, and the same pages carry the worked example of cognitive parallax. He calls it the one book whose literary structure he was able to compose deliberately, like a piece of music.

Caveat: He notes that his account converges with Antonio Negri's Empire, written from the opposite political direction, and does not treat the convergence as embarrassing. He also says the book's narrative stops at the American empire and that everything after it needed the later work — see the three projects of global domination.

He credits: Aristotle

The three projects of global domination Os três projetos de dominação global

The sequel to the Empire thesis, and the framework he is best known for outside philosophy: the forces now contesting world power are not one conspiracy but three separate projects of global domination — which he names provisionally the Russo-Chinese, the Western, and the Islamic — of comparable weight, sometimes allied and sometimes at each other's throats. He insists nobody can say which will prevail, including the people running them.

Where it comes from: He presents it as the continuation of O Jardim das Aflições, whose narrative stops at the American empire: the 2011 debate with Aleksandr Dugin is, in his words, the book's proper complement, carrying the analysis forward to the present.

He revised it: The naming shifts across the corpus. In 2009 he lists the communist project first and calls the third simply globalist; by 2013 the same three are 'the three globalisms'; and around 2020 he adds an observation rather than a correction, that the Western project is dismantling itself and the other two are collecting the winnings.

Caveat: He rejects the 'masters of the universe' reading his own audience often draws from this — the point of a three-way contest is that no one is in control. He also insists 'Anglo-American' is the wrong name for the Western project.

The triple intuition A tripla intuição

In any act of knowing, three things are grasped at once and cannot be separated: the act itself, the thing known, and the conditions that make the act possible. His model is sight — you never see an object without also seeing the light, and the light that shows the object is the same light that reaches the eye. He argues this connection is what the whole edifice of logic rests on, since a certainty available only in words could never underwrite a proof.

Where it comes from: He dates the idea to an argument with the position that there is no reality outside human language, and calls it, in the same breath, one of his great discoveries and one of the worst written things he ever produced — a draft that circulated as a course handout and was never made into a book.

Lineage: He built it to ground what he calls his radical intuitionism, and treats the near-universal symbolism of light and the sun as knowledge as evidence that the structure is not his invention but a description.

He credits: Aristotle Aquinas

Metacapitalism O metacapitalismo

A name for capital that has outgrown the market it came from: firms large enough to stop accepting the market's verdict and to want the state instead. He uses it to argue that world revolution is now promoted by the very rich rather than against them, and that the economy this produces — a strong state ringed by a handful of associated giants — is the arrangement he calls fascist, whatever the country's professed system.

Caveat: This is an economic characterisation, not a technical one; he does not present it as a contribution to economics and names no economist he is amending.

The revolutionary mentality A mentalidade revolucionária

Not a political position but a mental disorder with a structure, which he says has been running for four centuries: a mind that inverts the direction of time, treating a promised future as the cause of the present; inverts the subject-object relation, so the theory tells reality what it must be; and inverts moral responsibility, so the harm done in pursuit of the promise is charged to whoever resisted. He distinguishes it sharply from cognitive parallax, which is a defect of perception rather than a fully formed inversion.

Where it comes from: He traces the investigation to Joseph Gabel's work on false consciousness, read decades before he began, and says Gabel was on the right track without following it through.

He credits: Marx

The landings of philosophy A teoria dos patamares

Philosophy advances by landings rather than by schools: certain things, once seen, cannot be unseen, and reasoning below them is not disagreement but regression. Aristotle's distinction between form and matter is his standing example. The claim is normative, not historical — he says nobody has the right to argue beneath a landing, while granting that later work may fundamentally alter what stands on it.

Caveat: Asked in class for the list of landings, he agreed it would be worth having and did not produce one. The corpus records the criterion and the examples, not the inventory.

The subject of history A teoria do sujeito da história

History is only ever the history of agents that act, and most of the subjects historians write about cannot act. Brazil, he argues, has no history — it is the stage on which one happens, since a juridical-territorial unit has never done anything. The condition he says nobody had thought to state is continuity in time: an action exhausted inside one lifetime never enters history at all. What qualifies is a body that can train its successors to carry on a programme begun before they were born.

On the count: The list of kinds shifts between tellings. In 2009 he names the Catholic Church, Freemasonry, the Communist Party and dynastic families; in 2010 the great religions, initiatic societies, dynasties and the Communist Party; in 2011 he replaces the fourth with spiritual entities — God, angels and demons — and states there are no others. The four shown here are the human register, on which every telling agrees.

Where it comes from: He says he had to settle the question before he could ask whether historical knowledge explains anything at all about the divisions he was trying to describe, and calls the result an entirely new theory of history which he believes correct and irrefutable.

Lineage: Dynasties are the case he develops furthest, and the one that carries the most weight in his political writing: aristocratic or plebeian families conscious of the durability of their own interests, who take children from the cradle and raise them to continue a programme — the Bourbons and the Tudors, and, on his reading, the Rockefellers and the Rothschilds. What makes a dynasty an agent is fidelity to dead mentors. He then treats the Bilderberg circle as a consortium of such dynasties rather than as an organisation, which is how this doctrine does the work his politics asks of it.

Caveat: The fourth kind, in the fullest telling, is not human: he counts God, angels and demons as historical agents on the same criterion, since they too persist and act. Recorded as his claim. He also says himself that the theory has a problem — he never managed to apply it in a book that was entirely about history.

The test of the ambiguous contradictory O teste da contraditória ambígua

A test he proposes for statements that are neither plainly true nor plainly false — 'I am here', 'A equals A' — where the answer changes with the sense in which the terms are taken. He treats it as a practical exercise in noticing which sense one is actually using.

The collective imbecile O imbecil coletivo

His most cited coinage, and by his own account not an insult about intelligence. The collective imbecile is not made of imbeciles: it is normal and sometimes very able people who, out of professional solidarity or political convenience, adopt habits of language that stop them thinking, and who make one another stupid by doing it together.

Caveat: He never calls it a theory, and it is not presented as one here: the book is a collection of examples from Brazilian intellectual life, gathered from the late 1980s onward. It is in this panel because he defines the mechanism explicitly and returns to it as a concept.

The juvenile imbecile O imbecil juvenil

An observation rather than a theory, and one his readers took up as a working category: the adolescent who believes he is rebelling is aiming at the weakest authority available — parents who love him and will do anything for him — while submitting completely to the strongest, the peers who will not. He puts the mechanism underneath it as a neurotic need for affection that produces group dependence, and says the political and moral language such a person uses is decoration over a much cruder motive.

On the count: It is not an age. He is explicit that juvenile imbeciles are sixty and seventy years old: the essay describes a condition people carry into old age, not a phase they leave.

Where it comes from: A short article — he says he compressed the whole thing into a page and a half — published on his own site and later placed first in O Mínimo que Você Precisa Saber para Não Ser um Idiota (2013). He cites it in class the way one cites a reference, three times across four years, rather than re-explaining it.

Lineage: He connects it to the American literature on peer pressure, which he says runs to thousands of books there and nothing at all in Brazil. It is also the illustration he reaches for when defining existential contradiction.

Caveat: He never calls it a theory, and it is not one here. It earns its place as a described observation with an unusually clear trace of uptake: students bring it back to him unprompted across the whole run of the course — a parent asking how to keep small children from becoming juvenile imbeciles, a student connecting it to his exchange with the theologian Carlos Josaphat — using the phrase as a category rather than as a title.

Existential contradiction A contradição existencial

A contradiction not between two statements but inside a life: someone whose existence rests on an authority he is simultaneously trying to undermine — which, he argues, means the undermining is not fully serious, because the attack presupposes what it attacks. He sets it against merely logical contradiction, which he says any author will commit by inattention and which it is adolescent to go hunting for.

Lineage: His illustration is the juvenile imbecile: the boy who is brave towards the parents who love him and subservient to the peer group that does not.

Law as guarantee O direito como garantia

His position in philosophy of law: that law is to be understood as guarantee — a limit protecting the person — rather than as a command issued by whoever holds power. He treats it as following from the four discourses, since what a legal text can establish depends on the kind of discourse it is.

Where it comes from: Set out in a course handout of his, 'O que é o direito', which he never developed into a book of his own.

Lineage: The most externally attested item in this panel, and the only one whose development is somebody else's: Luiz Vergílio Dalla-Rosa built it into a doctoral thesis and a published book, O Direito como garantia: pressupostos de uma teoria constitucional (América Jurídica, 2002). He describes the thesis as departing from his handout and as his own position deepened by another hand.

Caveat: The claim of paternity is his. This project has not read the Dalla-Rosa volume and does not adjudicate how much of it is his; the attribution is recorded as his statement, not as a finding.

Parallel storylines

The chronology read as parallel storylines: one row per lane, one column per decade, each cell the number of that lane’s events in that decade. An event that belongs to several storylines is counted in each, so the rows sum to more than the number of events. Select a number to jump to that decade in the chronology.

These lanes are an editorial reading of a contested life, not a neutral fact: the currents are the ones he himself named as his formation — in a 2012 lecture he called it his great sin to have spent 'thirty-some years absorbing all the influences' (per the community transcriptions) — plus the public roles the record documents. Lanes are containers for dated events, never a claim that the currents weigh the same. Disambiguation & nuance

Almost everything about this subject is contested terrain — his title, his influence, his death, and his own retellings of his trajectory. This section records the distinctions the sources themselves make.

olavodecarvalho.org is no longer his site

As of August 2026 the live domain returns a page of unrelated e-commerce spam (apparently lapsed and squatted). His actual site must be cited via Internet Archive snapshots (e.g. the 15 December 2021 capture). Do not link the live domain as an official site.[34]

'Philosopher' is a claimed and contested title

He styled himself a philosopher and taught philosophy for decades, but held no degree in the field (he left PUC-Rio's Conpefil without completing studies); mainstream obituaries write 'self-described philosopher', his students insist on the title without qualification. This site records who calls him what.[41] [4]

Two works named O Jardim das Aflições

The 1995 book (Diadorim) and the 2017 documentary about him (dir. Josias Teófilo) share the name; AdoroCinema additionally mislabels the film as 2015.[42]

Not the other Carvalhos

Not the historian José Murilo de Carvalho (d. 2023), the poet Ronald de Carvalho, or the politician Salatiel Carvalho — search results routinely conflate them.[40]

'Guru of Bolsonaro' is an attributed label

The international press (NYT: 'Bolsonaro's Far-Right Guru') and much of the Brazilian press used the label; Olavo publicly rejected it, and the scholar Mark Sedgwick considers it overstated. It appears on this site only as someone's characterization, never in the site's own voice.[8] [30]

The COF corpus is cited, not reproduced

The Curso Online de Filosofia's community transcriptions (585 lectures) are public and of varying review quality, and the lecture header dates carry known defects. This project cites lectures by aula number and date only; the reception index in data/cof-reception.json records which lectures engage which philosopher.[37] [38]
