About

Work-in-progress dataset compiled from public sources (see references). Who
Olavo Luiz Pimentel de Carvalho — self-taught philosopher and polemicist, from 2009 teacher of the Curso Online de Filosofia (COF); earlier a journalist, and a professional astrologer around 1979–1982[1] [9] [29]
Born
29 April 1947, Campinas, São Paulo, Brazil[1] [9] [40]
Died
Night of 24 January 2022, in a hospital in the Richmond, Virginia area (USA), aged 74; announced by the family in the early hours of 25 January. The cause was publicly disputed — see the chronology[1] [2] [4] [8]
Residency
Brazilian citizen; resident in the United States (Richmond, Virginia area) from 2005 until his death; buried at St. Joseph's Cemetery, Petersburg, Virginia[9] [1] [39]
Decorations
Medalha do Pacificador (Brazilian Army, 25 August 1999); Comendador of Romania's Ordinul naţional Pentru Merit (by decree of President Emil Constantinescu, 5 December 2000); Medalha Mérito Santos Dumont (Brazilian Air Force, 20 July 2001); Grã-Cruz of the Ordem de Rio Branco (2019 — contested, see chronology). The two Brazilian days and months are his own CV's; the Romanian date is the decree's.[1] [55] [86] [10] [23]
Family
Married to Roxane Andrade de Souza; eight children and eighteen grandchildren at his death[1] [9]

Chronology

Key events in chronological order. A ? flag marks dates not yet verified against a primary source.

YearDatePlaceEvent
1940s
1947 1947-04-29 Campinas, SP, Brazil Born in Campinas, São Paulo — Born Olavo Luiz Pimentel de Carvalho. Obituaries note he had little formal schooling and described himself as self-taught.[1] [9] [40]
1950s
1954 c. 1947–1954 ? Brazil Childhood illness — his own account — His most repeated origin story in the course: sick from birth and bedridden through his first seven years with constant fever, then suddenly and inexplicably cured around age seven, going straight to school afterward (told, among other places, in the lectures of 9 October 2010 and 20 April 2013). In a lecture of 16 January 2010 he added that he was born with an undiagnosed tracheal tumor still with him in his sixties; in a lecture of c. 2020 he tied his late-diagnosed emphysema to a lung operation decades earlier. All of this is his own retrospective testimony via the community transcriptions, unverified against audio — recorded here as testimony, not as a medical record. A second line of his own testimony describes the condition itself, twice. On 10 August 2009 he said he had been born with a tracheal tumour and an infectious bronchitis that was near-fatal for seven years — though a fortnight later he said instead that nobody had known what he had, a wobble worth recording rather than smoothing. Again on his programme of 14 April 2010 — eight years before the surgery and twelve before his death — he said he had been born with a tracheal tumour, which he sized by comparison to an orange. In the same passage he said he had smoked since fifteen, three packs a day for over forty years. Both are his own statements in machine-transcribed broadcasts and neither is a medical record; the first is the earliest first-person account yet found of the congenital condition that the 2018 surgery would remove, and it long predates the disputes that followed his death.[37] [76]
1960s
1966 1966 ? São Paulo, Brazil Brief affiliation with the Brazilian Communist Party (PCB) — A BBC Brasil profile built on an in-person interview (Fellet, December 2016) states 'Entre 1966 e 1968, militou no Partido Comunista'; the historian Gilberto Calil records a brief PCB passage in the 1960s without exact dates. The dating rests on his own recollection as relayed by the interviewer, so the flag stays. In the course he bracketed the period himself: in 1964 he was a secondary student with vague leftist sympathy, not yet a militant, and he recalled joining the party 'to stir up trouble' and spending twenty years working the influence out of himself (lectures of 2009 and 2014, per the community transcriptions).[56] [29] [41] [37]
1970s
1977 1977-02 São Paulo, Brazil First contributions to Folha de S.Paulo — Both Wikipedias date his first Folha de S.Paulo piece (in the Folhetim section, on The Magic Flute) to February 1977. Other sources push his journalism back earlier — Gazeta do Povo says he started at 17 at Folha da Manhã; Calil says he began writing for major newspapers in 1967. The sources do not reconcile these, and they may describe different roles. His own telling in the course: working from age 14 to ease his mother's burden, and entering journalism at 17 expressly because the legal five-hour day left time to study (lecture of 13 November 2010, among others), with the professional registry later obtained by time of service.[40] [41] [9] [37]
1979 1979 São Paulo, Brazil Co-founds the Escola Júpiter de Astrologia — Worked professionally as an astrologer c. 1979–1982, co-founding the school and its Revista de Astrologia Júpiter and collaborating on a university-extension astrology course at PUC-SP; Calil (2021) reports the school had about 140 students by 1980. He later abandoned and disavowed astrology. His own telling in the course: professionally an astrologer for about two years, with some 5,000 charts interpreted — psychiatrists sent him patients' horoscopes — and the school closing around 1980; he quit after judging the techniques unserious.[41] [40] [29] [9] [37]
1980s
1980 1980 ? São Paulo, Brazil First book: A Imagem do Homem na Astrologia — The 1980 date belongs to a 38-page booklet (Ed. Júpiter), grown out of the 1979 PUC-SP astrology course. Olavo's own CV opens its 'Livros publicados' list with three 1983 titles (Speculum) — which explains Agência Brasil's 'first book, 1983': the two dates describe different things (a course booklet vs. his first books proper). The 1980 edition is attested only by book-trade listings, so the flag stays.[41] [1] [55]
1982 1982 ? Brazil Enters the Traditionalist / Perennialist milieu — Per Calil (2021), around 1982 he joined an esoteric 'Tradition' group. Sedgwick's peer-reviewed history of Brazilian Traditionalism (2021) documents the arc: an interest in Traditionalism from 1977, after he was given Jacob Needleman's anthology The Sword of Gnosis; Michel Veber's 1980 lecture on Guénon at the Escola Júpiter and a further series in 1981; his own translation of Guénon's La Métaphysique orientale (A Metafísica Oriental, Escola Júpiter, 1981); an article in the New Age magazine Planeta (no. 107, August 1981) calling Veber 'my professor' — in which he also wrote that Veber had studied under Guénon's personal guidance, which Sedgwick notes was impossible, Veber having been four years old when Guénon left France in 1930; the founding of an Instituto de Estudos Tradicionais; and the first Traditionalist article to appear in a major Brazilian newspaper, 'Moralidade sem Deus?' (Jornal da Tarde, 27 February 1982). He also led followers into A Tradição, the organization of Omar Ali Shah, and broke with it after concluding its leader was a 'con-man' (Sedgwick, quoting his term) — a public dispute that reached the police. Approximate dating for the group entry itself; all characterizations are these scholars'.[29] [30] [75]
1984 1984 ? Brazil Involvement with Sufi Islam (the Maryamiyya of Frithjof Schuon) — Calil (2021) states he converted to Islam in 1984, took the name Sidi Mohammad Ibrahim, and set up a tariqa of his own that dissolved within months. Sedgwick (2021) documents the sequence from the Lings correspondence: after contacting Marco Pallis, Carvalho and some of his followers corresponded with Martin Lings, who explained that 'entering Islam' was the appropriate preparation for the Maryami tariqa — which they did. Lings's letters address him as 'Sidi Muhammad' from June 1985; they were deposited at the São Paulo state court in 1990 as evidence in a case brought by a former follower, which Carvalho won. Sedgwick places him at the 1985 Traditionalist conference in Lima where Lings was present, but marks that presence as deduced rather than documented; he records that in 1986 Carvalho led a group of Brazilians to Bloomington, Indiana, where Schuon appointed him muqaddam (Sufi representative) for Brazil. Carvalho himself later said he had 'entered' Islam rather than converted, in Guénon's sense. On the exit Sedgwick is only relative — 'within a few years' he became disenchanted, telling an interviewer he had discovered that Schuon backed a rival for the leadership of the Brazilian Maryamiyya and had endorsed rumours against him — which converges with his own telling in the course: met Schuon in 1986, made muqaddam on arrival, stayed about a year and a half, provoked his own expulsion, placing the exit around 1987–88 (per the community transcriptions). He later disavowed the period's books as reflecting 'more a duty of obedience' to Schuon than his own thought.[29] [30] [75] [37]
1986 1986 São Paulo, Brazil Publishes Fronteiras da Tradição, his first book beyond astrology — Lectures from his Traditionalist teaching period — several first given at the Editora Astrociência in Rio de Janeiro — are published as Fronteiras da Tradição (São Paulo: Nova Stella, 1986), per Sedgwick (2021) his first book on a topic other than astrology. Its references are 'almost without exception' the major Traditionalist authors; it urges an orthodox religion as the exoteric frame for esoteric practice and warns against pseudo-initiatic organizations, citing A Tradição as an example. The volume also reprints the 1982 Jornal da Tarde article.[75]
1987 1987-03 ? Rio de Janeiro, Brazil The course his CV calls the seminar's beginning — His curriculum vitae lists a course called “Introdução à Vida Intelectual”, taught from March 1987 to March 1989 at the Instituto Sto. André in Rio de Janeiro, and adds that under the name Seminário Permanente de Filosofia e Humanidades it “continues to this day” in São Paulo. That is his own claim of an unbroken teaching line, and it is the only dated origin the seminar has: no independent record of a founding has been found, and the organization entry keeps the date unverified. It sits beside rather than against the “teaching since 1989” he gives in the online course — the Rio course ends in March 1989 and the renamed seminar takes over from there, so the two self-datings mark different starting points. The sentence carrying the claim is undated, like the rest of the document: the CV's sections were revised at different times, so “to this day” dates from whenever that paragraph was last touched. Its list of internal course handouts — forty-two fascicles under the Seminário Permanente name — is the only other trace of the course in it.[55] [37]
1990s
1994 1994 Rio de Janeiro, Brazil Publishes A Nova Era e a Revolução Cultural — On Gramsci and Fritjof Capra (Instituto de Artes Liberais); obituaries treat it as the book that established him as a conservative polemicist.[40] [9] [29]
1995 1995 Rio de Janeiro, Brazil Publishes O Jardim das Aflições — Subtitled 'de Epicuro à ressurreição de César' (Diadorim); later republished, and the source of the 2017 documentary's title.[40] [9]
1996 1996 Rio de Janeiro, Brazil Publishes O Imbecil Coletivo — His polemic against the Brazilian intelligentsia ('atualidades inculturais brasileiras'); Calil (2021) notes it achieved significant circulation and made him a public figure.[9] [29] [41]
1996 1996 Rio de Janeiro, Brazil Publishes Aristóteles em Nova Perspectiva — His 'theory of the four discourses' reading of Aristotle (Topbooks); year and publisher rest on a single tertiary source.[40]
1999 1999-08-25 ? Brazil Receives the Medalha do Pacificador (Brazilian Army) — His curriculum vitae dates the medal to 25 August 1999 and names the Brazilian Army Command as the conferring authority. Maria Cristina Fernandes, writing in Valor Econômico in April 2019, puts it in the same year as a lecture he gave to a military audience and attributes it to the then army minister, General Gleuber Vieira, who she reports had also written to him praising his work as “intelligent, prudent, balanced and full of good sense”. She does not name the lecture; the only military one his CV dates to 1999 is “Reparando uma injustiça pessoal” at the Clube Militar in Rio de Janeiro on 31 March. Agência Brasil's obituary career summary records the medal without a date.[1] [55] [88]The year is in Agência Brasil's obituary and the circumstances are in Valor Econômico; the exact day, 25 August 1999, comes from his own CV and has not been checked against an Army bulletin.
2000s
2000 2000 Porto Alegre, RS, Brazil Speaks at the 13th Fórum da Liberdade — His earliest surviving recorded appearance. The edition's theme was the Brazilian quincentenary — 'Mil anos: o Brasil em perspectiva' — and he argued that a country reflecting on five hundred years of history had collapsed the question into a quarrel about present economic policy. He named the gap a parallax, borrowing the term from photography, and applied it to the lettered class rather than to an individual. Olívio Dutra, then governor of Rio Grande do Sul, had spoken from the same platform, and Carvalho answered him by name while calling him honest. The recording does not state the edition number; it is fixed by the Forum's own list of editions.[80] [78]
2000 2000-12-05 Romania Made a Commander of Romania's Ordinul naţional Pentru Merit — Decree 581 of 5 December 2000, signed by President Emil Constantinescu and countersigned by Prime Minister Mugur Isărescu, confers the Ordinul naţional Pentru Merit on a list of Brazilian and Romanian recipients “in recognition of the contribution made to the development of Romanian-Brazilian relations”. In the rank of Comandor, between an art collector and an honorary consul, stands “domnului Olavo de Carvalho, profesor universitar” — a description the decree supplies and that appears nowhere in his own curriculum vitae. The decree was published in Monitorul Oficial I no. 648 of 12 December 2000, and Romanian press coverage of his death cited it again in January 2022. His CV records the same decoration and the same date, and documents the Romanian connection behind it: a paper at the Latinité et Nouvel Ordre Mondial congress in Cluj-Napoca in July 1998, Bucharest radio and press interviews in late 1998, and a lecture on twentieth-century Brazilian philosophers at the Casa de América Latina in Bucharest on 8 September 1999.[86] [87] [55]
2001 2001-07-20 ? Brazil Receives the Medalha Mérito Santos Dumont (Brazilian Air Force) — Recorded without a date in Agência Brasil's obituary career summary. His own curriculum vitae dates it to 20 July 2001 and names the Comando da Aeronáutica as the conferring authority.[1] [55]The year is in Agência Brasil's obituary; the exact day, 20 July 2001, comes from his own CV and is not independently confirmed.
2002 2002 Brazil Founds the website Mídia Sem Máscara — A media-criticism site against what it described as leftist bias in the Brazilian press; Calil (2021) treats it as his main ideological platform from 2002 until it went offline around 2017.[29] [40] [41] [92]The .org domain was registered 24 July 2002 and the site was serving content by 11 August, which is an independent bound on the founding year rather than a restatement of it.
2002 2002 Curitiba and São Paulo, Brazil Records the Curso História Essencial da Filosofia — already its fifth delivery — An eight-session history-of-philosophy course, videotaped by the publisher É Realizações, running from the pre-Socratics to the advent of Christianity. He dates it on the recordings themselves and says he has already taught it four times, the first in Rio in 1993 — so this is a fifth delivery of a course a decade old, not a new one. It is taught live and in person, in the same room as his separate Seminário de Filosofia, which he mentions meets the following day: the seminar therefore existed in Brazil three years before he emigrated. The recordings are the fullest systematic statement of his reading of the philosophers before the Curso Online de Filosofia begins in 2009, and the printed collection drawn from them runs to 2006.[77]
2002 2002 Porto Alegre, RS, Brazil Speaks at the 15th Fórum da Liberdade, on the future of democracy — On the edition's theme, 'Os desafios da democracia no século XXI'. He set out three dangers to democracy: Gramsci's hegemony and expanded state, sold under the name of organised civil society; the extension of democracy past the political and juridical domain, which he says turns it into its opposite; and a third he named himself, liberal economicism. The recording does not state the edition number, and predates the October presidential election — the floor questions refer to that year's candidates as still running.[81] [78]
2004 2004 Porto Alegre, RS, Brazil Debates Eduardo Bueno on culture and development at the 17th Fórum da Liberdade — The moderator names the edition from the stage. Bueno gave the sixteenth century; Carvalho gave the present, arguing that the Brazilian constitutional definition of culture — whatever bears witness to a mode of existence — puts culture at the antipodes of cultivation and cult, and that no burst of philosophical creativity in history has followed an economic boom. He named Mário Ferreira dos Santos as a philosopher of world rank whom Brazil had left out of print.[82] [78]
2005 2005 Virginia, USA Moves to the United States — Settled in the Richmond, Virginia area, where he lived until his death; the date is given consistently across obituaries and profiles.[9] [1] [10] [40]
2005 2005 Porto Alegre, RS, Brazil Shares a platform with Oded Grajew at the 18th Fórum da Liberdade — Announced from the stage as the eighteenth edition, on 'A cultura do trabalho', with the Sri Lankan-Australian jurist Suri Ratnapala and Oded Grajew, a founder of the World Social Forum, whose Porto Alegre meetings this Forum was founded against. Grajew left before Carvalho's turn. Carvalho described a seventy-year cycle of state intervention in labour relations, then reframed the debate as an asymmetry of forces rather than a contest of programmes — naming the industrialist Jorge Gerdau and a handful of businessmen on one side and, on the other, the FARC, the Foro de São Paulo, the European Union, the United Nations and the great American foundations. Months later he emigrated.[83] [78] [79]
2005 2005-09-15 ? Arlington, VA, USA First dated lecture to a Washington audience, at the Atlas Foundation — Four months after he settled in Virginia, his curriculum vitae records a talk titled “Brazilian left: from victory to defeat to victory again” at the Atlas Foundation for Economic Studies in Arlington, Virginia. His English-language résumé gives the same date to a talk at the “Atlas Economic Research Foundation” in Washington D.C., and adds a second talk eight days later at Georgetown University's Center for Latin American Studies on 23 September 2005 that the Portuguese CV omits altogether. Both documents are his own; no programme, report or recording of either occasion has been found.[55] [90]
2006 2006-02-19 ? Arlington, VA, USA The São Paulo Forum thesis, delivered to the Intelligence Summit — His curriculum vitae records a talk titled “Neocommunist rise: The São Paulo Forum and the Strategical Unity of the Latin-American Left” at the Intelligence Summit 2006 in Arlington, Virginia, on 19 February 2006 — the earliest dated presentation of his São Paulo Forum thesis to a United States audience that any of his own documents record. The occasion itself is independently documented: ABC News, reporting on 14 February 2006 that a set of Saddam Hussein recordings would be released there, described “a nongovernmental meeting — called Intelligence Summit 2006 — this weekend in Arlington, Va.”, which puts the conference on the weekend of 17–19 February. What is not documented anywhere outside his own paperwork is that he was on the programme. His English-language résumé dates the same talk to 19 February 2007 in Washington D.C. — one more reminder that a curriculum vitae cannot settle a question about itself.[55] [89] [90]The conference is externally dated and located; his appearance on its programme rests on his own CV, whose English counterpart dates the same talk a year later.
2006 2006-09-12 ? Washington, DC, USA “Towards a diagnosis of the Latin American political situation”, at the America's Future Foundation — The last of the three United States talks his curriculum vitae dates, and the one his two self-written curricula agree on: both place it at the America's Future Foundation in Washington D.C. on 12 September 2006. With the Atlas talk of September 2005 and the Intelligence Summit paper of February 2006 it marks the other half of the emigration — the Brazilian polemicist becoming a speaker to American audiences about Latin America — a few months before True Outspeak began broadcasting. Nothing beyond his own documents attests any of the three.[55] [90]
2006 2006-12-04 USA (online) Begins the online radio program True Outspeak — First episode aired 4 December 2006 — preserved with the show's own audio and a transcript ('estamos inaugurando o nosso programa') in an Internet Archive collection; Calil (2021) confirms the program. It ran on BlogTalkRadio until 2013 per English Wikipedia.[43] [29] [40]
2007 2007 ? Richmond area, Virginia, USA A student lives in the house, and describes the working week — Ítalo Marsili's account, told years later on a podcast: he arrived in his early twenties with a one-way ticket and no money, having gone bankrupt, and gave the wrong flight time — Carvalho went to the airport eight hours early, waited, went home, worked out the real arrival and came back for him. On the lot stood a large outbuilding holding the office and the library, shelved as literature, theology and philosophy. The household lived on roughly 200 dollars from the Jornal do Brasil column; Marsili says he barely ate and began smoking to fill the gap. The week had a shape: talk ran until seven in the morning and the house slept through the day; on Wednesdays Carvalho read that week's newspaper article aloud to everyone in the office and they argued about it; on Mondays Marsili sorted the listeners' questions into a file for the radio programme. Testimony, and one person's, given long after the fact — the working week it describes is corroborated by the broadcasts, the poverty is not corroborated anywhere else.[85]The year is placed by what the account describes — the Jornal do Brasil column and the weekly web radio programme with listener questions — not by any date given in it.
2007 2007-04 ? São Paulo, Brazil (taught from the United States) Teaching Brazil by videoconference from the United States — His own telling on the True Outspeak broadcasts of April 2007: the Seminário de Filosofia he had been running in Brazil since at least 2002 was by then reaching São Paulo by videoconference from the United States, hosted at É Realizações, and he announced a session for the 18th of that month. In February 2007 he put the teaching in a longer line — courses 'for twenty years', which he said he was continuing by videoconference — and listeners wrote in as students. What changed after the 2005 emigration was therefore the delivery, not the teaching: the Curso Online de Filosofia of 2009 industrialized an operation that had already crossed the Atlantic. Recorded as self-testimony from machine-transcribed broadcasts, not as an institutional record.[76]
2007 2007-07-07 USA (online) His YouTube channel is created — The channel's own About page records 'Joined Jul 7, 2007' — a primary source that contradicts Portuguese Wikipedia's 2015 launch claim (2015 may reflect when regular use began, but no source supports it). The channel passed one million subscribers by his death.[52] [41]
2008 2008-08-04 ? Online (from Virginia, USA) seminariodefilosofia.org goes live — as an archive, not a course — He opens the site on air, in the middle of the broadcast, describing it as a division of his own site. What it is for is the back catalogue: for years people had been asking for recordings and handouts of his old courses, and he says the material runs to more than twenty years of classes — some filmed, some on audio, some transcribed into apostilas, most of them never corrected by him and therefore provisional. Subscribers pay a monthly fee, five or six items are added a month, and the previous month's material moves to an archive that stays downloadable on an undertaking not to redistribute. The live course would follow seven months later. Self-testimony from a machine-transcribed broadcast, paraphrased.[76]
2009 2009 USA Founds the Inter-American Institute for Philosophy, Government, and Social Thought — Founded in late 2009 (its web domain was registered 10 December 2009 and it operated as a Virginia 501(c)(3), per IRS records); Olavo served as president. He announced its closure himself on 29 November 2018 ('Fechei o Interamerican...'), reported by the press days later.[44] [45] [46] [93]
2009 2009-03-14 Online (from Virginia, USA) First lecture of the Curso Online de Filosofia (COF) — The paid weekly online philosophy course he taught until his death — 585 lectures over thirteen years. A student index and English Wikipedia date the first class to 14 March 2009. The community transcription corpus of the course is public (see references); this project cites it by lecture number and date only. His own broadcasts bracket the launch closely: on 9 March 2009 he announced the first class for 'next Saturday, the 14th', and a week later reported that they had expected about a hundred people and more than four hundred arrived, collapsing the streaming provider and forcing the bandwidth to be widened. The start had slipped for five months — promised 'next month' in December 2008, then 'February, March at the latest' in January, then 'a week, ten days at most' in late February — and the delivered format was a members-only stream with an open Brazilian phone line rather than the Skype arrangement he had described in October 2008. It began fortnightly and was weekly by that August.[36] [40] [37] [76]
2009 2009-08-17 ? Online (from Virginia, USA) A Mente Revolucionária at 550 pages, and the reason it was slow — Asked on air how the book was coming, he answers that he has written 550 pages and is barely half way through the subject, because each part carries its own documentation problem. The constraint he gives is time, and the list is a snapshot of his week five months after the COF launched: he has the programme to make, the Seminário de Filosofia to look after and newspaper articles to write, leaving one or two days for the book — much slower than he had expected, and he adds that he no longer has the wind he once had, when a night's sitting produced seventy pages. Self-testimony from a machine-transcribed broadcast, paraphrased; the book had not appeared by his death.[76]
2010s
2010 2010-02-22 ? Virginia, USA Says he now holds a green card, ending the visa constraint — He mentions it first in passing while signing off — he has his green card now, and the country he left has been stolen from him. Three later broadcasts of 2010 fill it in: he was admitted as a resident alien of extraordinary ability, the category he says is given to great scientists; it took four years of work and a thousand pages of documents; and he uses it in arguing for the Arizona immigration law. This bears on the shape of the emigration: the constraint under which he left Brazil in 2005 has an end date, and it falls here. Self-testimony from machine-transcribed broadcasts, paraphrased and dated to them; no immigration record has been sought or seen.[76]
2010 2010-10-30 Online (from Virginia, USA) Names Padre Paulo Ricardo as his personal confessor — In the lecture of 30 October 2010 he described Padre Paulo Ricardo de Azevedo as his personal confessor; a year earlier (28 November 2009) he had called him the only priest in Brazil he trusted, and in May 2010 he recounted the priest's recent visit to his Virginia home. Together with his stated practice of confession as late as 2020 and the sacraments received on his deathbed (reported by Silvio Grimaldo of Brasil Sem Medo), this documents in his own dated words his return to full Catholic practice during the US years. The origin of that return — accounts circulate of a c. 2006 visit — remains unverified and is told differently by sympathetic and hostile sources.[37] [58]
2011 2011-01-24 ? Online (from Virginia, USA) Announces the Dugin debate, six weeks before it opens — He tells his listeners that a group of his students in Bahia is organising an internet debate between him and Aleksandr Dugin, whom he describes as the author of the Eurasian strategy and a kind of guru to Putin — and as a man of extraordinary talent and great culture, intellectually worthy of every respect. The format is set before the date is: written, on a site built for the purpose, with the text reproduced on his own site, on Mídia Sem Máscara, on the Inter-American Institute's site and wherever Dugin chooses. The question is which forces are contending for world power and what part the United States plays in it. No date is fixed, but he has been told Dugin is already writing his first piece. Self-testimony, paraphrased; the debate opened on 4 March.[76]
2011 2011-03-04 Online Written debate with Aleksandr Dugin begins — The Carvalho–Dugin debate on 'The USA and the New World Order' ran online from 4 March to 31 July 2011, when both published their conclusions; the host site was reportedly accessed by some 200,000 people from over thirty countries. Sedgwick (2021) reads it as a debate about global politics rather than doctrine: Dugin began it believing he was debating a fellow Brazilian Traditionalist and ended disappointed, while Carvalho showed no interest in common ground — 'the debate, then, was not really about Traditionalism at all'.[33] [11] [75]
2011 2011-04-20 ? Online (from Virginia, USA) True Outspeak stops without a farewell — The last episode preserved in the archive's capture of the series ends the way every other one does — the time is up, until next week, thank you. There is no farewell in it or in the five before it, and an unexplained five-week gap sits just behind it, between 16 February and 23 March. English Wikipedia has the programme running until 2013; the capture of the public playlist holds 201 episodes and stops here. Recorded as a property of what survives, not as an end date: whether it continued elsewhere is not settled by the material this project holds.[76]
2012 2012 Campinas, SP, Brazil The Dugin debate is published as Os EUA e a Nova Ordem Mundial — Portuguese edition of the debate (VIDE Editorial, trans. Giuliano Moraes); still sold by the official Seminário de Filosofia bookstore.[33] [35]
2013 2013 Rio de Janeiro, Brazil Publishes O Mínimo que Você Precisa Saber para Não Ser um Idiota — 193 press articles from 1997–2013, organized by Felipe Moura Brasil (Record). A best-seller: English Wikipedia cites c. 320,000 copies; Poder360 reports 400,000 combined with O Imbecil Coletivo by 2019 — the figures differ.[17] [40] [2]
2016 2016 ? Europe Students of the course hold the first European meeting of the seminar — A gathering of Brazilian students of the Curso Online de Filosofia living in or passing through Europe, with Carvalho joining from the United States. Asked about exile, he said he had escaped the disintegration it caused Solzhenitsyn because he still had his family and an audience of thousands in his own language, and that the exile a philosopher actually needs is temporal rather than geographical — leaving your own epoch to see it with the eyes of another. The year comes from the recording's own title; no date is announced in it.[84]
2017 2017 ? Online Mídia Sem Máscara goes offline — Per Calil (2021), the site operated 2002–2017.[29]Calil gives 2002–2017. The site was still serving on 3 January and 4 September 2018, so his end date is early by at least a year; the shutdown falls between September 2018 and 2021. The philosophers he referenced

The twenty philosophers most engaged by the Curso Online de Filosofia, by measured presence in the 585-lecture corpus. Each page carries the thinker's own life and works, and the record of which lectures engage him.

Aristóteles (384–322 BC)

Greek philosopher, disciple of Plato and tutor of Alexander of Macedon, founder of the Lyceum in Athens. His surviving treatises cover logic, physics, biology, metaphysics, ethics, politics, rhetoric and poetics, and shaped much of ancient, medieval and modern philosophy.

COF reception: present in 422 files; 189 lectures engage him.

Descartes (1596–1650)

French philosopher, mathematician and scientist, often described as the founder of modern philosophy. He created analytic geometry, formulated the first modern version of mind–body dualism, and proposed a method of systematic doubt as the foundation of knowledge.

COF reception: present in 261 files; 102 lectures engage him.

Platão (c. 428/427–348/347 BC)

Athenian philosopher, disciple of Socrates and teacher of Aristotle, founder of the Academy. His dialogues, which put into circulation themes such as the theory of Forms, the immortality of the soul and the just city, are among the founding texts of Western philosophy.

COF reception: present in 315 files; 114 lectures engage him.

Marx (1818–1883)

German philosopher, economist and revolutionary, author (with Friedrich Engels) of the Communist Manifesto and of Capital. His theory of history, his critique of political economy and the political movement he inspired profoundly marked the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

COF reception: present in 322 files; 105 lectures engage him.

Tomás de Aquino (c. 1224/25–1274)

Dominican friar, theologian and philosopher, the leading figure of thirteenth-century scholasticism. His synthesis of Aristotelian philosophy and Christian doctrine, set out above all in the Summa theologiae and the Summa contra gentiles, became a central reference of the Catholic tradition.

COF reception: present in 242 files; 82 lectures engage him.

Kant (1724–1804)

German philosopher, a central figure of modern philosophy, who spent his whole life in Königsberg. His 'critical philosophy', set out in the three Critiques, proposed that human reason legislates both for the knowledge of nature and for morality.

COF reception: present in 222 files; 75 lectures engage him.

Sócrates (469–399 BC)

Athenian philosopher who wrote nothing, known through the testimonies of Plato, Xenophon and Aristophanes. His practice of interrogating fellow citizens about the virtues, and his condemnation to death for impiety, made him a founding figure of Western philosophical ethics.

COF reception: present in 182 files; 57 lectures engage him.

René Guénon (1886–1951)

French metaphysician and writer, the principal formulator of the twentieth-century 'Traditionalist' or 'Perennialist' current. He wrote on Eastern metaphysics, symbolism, initiation and the critique of the modern world; he embraced Islam and lived his last twenty years in Cairo.

COF reception: present in 118 files; 32 lectures engage him.

Hegel (1770–1831)

German philosopher, the leading exponent of post-Kantian German idealism. He elaborated a system spanning logic, philosophy of nature and philosophy of spirit, with lasting influence on philosophy, political theory and the historiography of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

COF reception: present in 168 files; 32 lectures engage him.

Louis Lavelle (1883–1951)

French philosopher, one of the principal French metaphysicians of the twentieth century and a representative of the 'philosophie de l'esprit' current. Professor at the Sorbonne and the Collège de France, he developed an ontology of participation in being, articulated in the tetralogy La dialectique de l'éternel présent.

COF reception: present in 93 files; 30 lectures engage him.

Nietzsche (1844–1900)

German philosopher and cultural critic, trained as a classical philologist, who published intensely through the 1870s and 1880s. He is known for his radical critiques of traditional European morality, religion and philosophy, and for concepts such as the will to power, the eternal recurrence and the Übermensch. His productive career ended abruptly with the mental collapse of 1889.

COF reception: present in 156 files; 39 lectures engage him.

Leibniz (1646–1716)

German polymath — philosopher, mathematician, jurist and diplomat — counted among the great rationalists of the seventeenth century. He developed the differential and integral calculus independently of Newton and formulated a metaphysical system centered on the monads and pre-established harmony. He spent most of his career in the service of the House of Hanover.

COF reception: present in 144 files; 17 lectures engage him.

Mário Ferreira dos Santos (1907–1968)

Brazilian philosopher, translator and writer, creator of the system he named 'concrete philosophy'. In little more than fifteen years he published the monumental Enciclopédia de Ciências Filosóficas e Sociais, in dozens of volumes, working outside the university establishment. He fell into relative obscurity after his death and has been republished since the 2000s, with the rights to his work acquired by the publisher É Realizações.

COF reception: present in 141 files; 20 lectures engage him.

Santo Agostinho (354–430)

Bishop of Hippo and one of the most influential Christian thinkers of all time, the bridge between ancient philosophy and medieval thought. A professor of rhetoric converted to ascetic Christianity in 386, he left more than five million words of surviving writings, among them the Confessions and The City of God.

COF reception: present in 136 files; 19 lectures engage him.

Eric Voegelin (1901–1985)

German-American political philosopher, formed in Vienna, who fled Nazism in 1938 and made his career in the United States and Munich. He sought a comprehensive philosophy of man, society and history, and became known for his analysis of modern political movements as 'gnosticism'. His principal works include The New Science of Politics and the Order and History series.

COF reception: present in 132 files; 26 lectures engage him.

Husserl (1859–1938)

A mathematician by training and the founder of phenomenology, the movement that marked all of twentieth-century continental philosophy. He made his career at Halle, Göttingen and Freiburg, where Heidegger succeeded him. Of Jewish origin, he spent his last years excluded from German university life under the Nazi regime.

COF reception: present in 107 files; 24 lectures engage him.

Heidegger (1889–1976)

A central figure of twentieth-century European philosophy, author of Being and Time (1927) and renewer of the question of the meaning of being. Husserl's disciple and successor at Freiburg, he influenced existentialism, hermeneutics and deconstruction. His involvement with Nazism — the 1933–34 rectorate and party membership until 1945 — remains an object of controversy.

COF reception: present in 99 files; 28 lectures engage him.

Frithjof Schuon (1907–1998)

Swiss-German writer and metaphysician, the leading exponent of the perennialist (Traditionalist) school in the twentieth century alongside René Guénon and Ananda Coomaraswamy. Initiated into Sufism in Algeria in 1933, he directed from Europe — and, after 1980, from Bloomington, Indiana — the order that became known as the Maryamiyya, and wrote dozens of books on metaphysics, comparative religion and sacred art. The accounts of his life here follow chiefly his estate's site and the historian of Traditionalism Mark Sedgwick.

COF reception: present in 55 files; 14 lectures engage him.

Ortega y Gasset (1883–1955)

Spanish philosopher, essayist and intellectual leader, formed in German neo-Kantianism and creator of 'vital reason' ('I am I and my circumstance'). Professor of metaphysics in Madrid and founder of the Revista de Occidente, he became world-famous with The Revolt of the Masses. He left Spain with the Civil War and returned after the Second World War.

COF reception: present in 82 files; 7 lectures engage him.

Dante Alighieri (c. 1265–1321)

Poet, political thinker and 'father of the Italian language', author of the Divine Comedy, the poetic synthesis of medieval culture. He took an active part in the civic life of Florence until his condemnation to exile in 1302, and wrote in banishment his great prose works and the poem that immortalized him. He died in Ravenna in 1321.

COF reception: present in 83 files; 6 lectures engage him.

Fílon de Alexandria (c. 25–10 BCE – c. 45–50 CE)

Hellenistic Jewish philosopher of Alexandria, who read the Pentateuch allegorically and in Greek philosophical terms, and whose Logos became a load-bearing idea for later Christian theology. Almost nothing in his life is securely dated: one embassy anchors the whole biography, and the standard references disagree about his birth by thirty years.

The twelve layers of personality As doze camadas da personalidade

A scheme of twelve demands that structure a human life, from taking possession of one's own body to standing before eternity. Each layer is a distinct kind of interest that can organise a person's whole existence, and he holds that most adults halt at the eighth, the crisis of maturity.

  1. 1The body
  2. 2The circle of the instincts
  3. 3Social relations and language
  4. 4Historically consolidated feelings
  5. 5Self-assertion
  6. 6Practical life
  7. 7Citizenship
  8. 8The crisis of maturity
  9. 9The intellectual personality
  10. 10Shaping social force
  11. 11Historical consciousness
  12. 12The final destiny

Where it comes from: Built in his Astrocaracterologia course (1990–92) from the twelve houses of the zodiac, taken as an ideal developmental circuit and grafted onto Szondi's drive psychology — which he said lacked any biographical dimension. He states three times that the scheme does not depend on astrology: he devised it to isolate precisely what a horoscope cannot describe.

Lineage: What moves a person between layers, in the class devoted to the scheme, is symbolic material: someone with no culture to think with cannot articulate what the crisis of maturity is doing to them, and so cannot pass out of it — the effort of articulation is itself what carries them across. He puts the intellectual personality on the far side of that crisis, and adds that what began as one person's private trouble becomes useful to others precisely because articulating it gives voice to people who could not. That class is also the strongest evidence for his repeated insistence that the scheme does not depend on astrology: across its full length neither astrology, nor the zodiac, nor Szondi is mentioned once.

He revised it: Presented as an evolutionary sequence in 2010 and 2014, then corrected in a later lecture: it is not an ideal development but the real structure of human existence, with all twelve demands pressing at once. He then distinguished the layer a person occupies from the stage that weighs on them. The diagram shows twelve simultaneous demands for that reason.

He credits: Guénon Hegel Aquinas

The selves, and the immortality of the soul Os eus e a imortalidade da alma

Knowledge by presence Conhecimento por presença

Beneath perception, memory and theory there is a prior, non-representational cognition that consists in reality simply being present to the knower. He calls it the most exact form of knowledge precisely because it cannot be turned into a thought one possesses. Its work is to explain how anyone ever corrects a framework from inside it: without it, he argues, a powerful philosophy would hypnotise its adherents completely and no school could revise itself.

Lineage: He names Suhrawardī and the Persian's Philosophy of Illumination — but says he had grasped the thing before reading him, that Suhrawardī used the phrase not quite in his sense, and the attribution appears only seven months after he begins using the term. Cognitive parallax Paralaxe cognitiva

A displacement between the axis of a thinker's lived experience and the axis of his theoretical construction — seeing one thing while theorising another. He claims the phenomenon as his own discovery and insists it is a cognitive defect rather than hypocrisy or lying.

Where it comes from: The worked case is older than the term's exposition in the course: he points out that he had already given it in O Jardim das Aflições (1995) — Marx announcing a materialist philosophy and delivering one whose real substance is human action, with nature entering only as raw material. He generalises from it that a philosophy built on a parallax is not so much false as unrealisable, so its author ends up doing a third thing neither he nor his readers can name.

Lineage: He takes the word from photography, where parallax is the divergence between the viewfinder and the lens — what you frame is not what the film records. The earliest located use is public rather than pedagogical, and applies the term to a class rather than to a thinker: at the Fórum da Liberdade in 2000 he diagnosed a country marking five hundred years of history by arguing about the present month's economic policy as a parallax, an abyss between life and consciousness. From there he draws the corollary he keeps: everyone in the debate reasons from the position of whoever decides, and reasoning from a position that is not really yours is what alienation means. The remedy he proposed was an autobiographical method — intellectuals asking not why Brazil went wrong but why they themselves did.

He revised it: He narrowed it by 2013 to strict performative self-refutation — where the act of saying something contradicts what is said — explicitly excluding mere self-contradiction, lying and propaganda, and presenting the narrowing as a correction of how his coinage was being used.

He credits: Marx Descartes Kant

The theory of the four discourses A teoria dos quatro discursos

His most widely deployed framework: four kinds of discourse — poetic, rhetorical, dialectical and analytical — graded by the certainty they can carry, which he treats as one scale rather than four separate disciplines. The order is a dependency, not a preference: he calls it the natural sequence of a human education, in which one learns to imagine and express the world before arguing about conduct, and argues about conduct before demonstrating anything. He read the Aristotelian corpus in that order and held that Aristotle's logic becomes unintelligible once it is amputated from his rhetoric.

The theory of Empire A teoria do Império

Empire is the recurring idea of Western history, and he traces its transmutations from the fall of Rome to the American expansion — each collapse followed by someone assembling the next one. His definition is the load-bearing part: an empire is a supranational organisation that does not federate nations but gathers forces from inside them, which is why it can operate against the interests of the very country that hosts it.

Where it comes from: Set out in O Jardim das Aflições (1995), of which he says a great part is a history of the transmutations of the idea of empire — a great part, not the whole: the book is subtitled an essay on materialism and civil religion, and the same pages carry the worked example of cognitive parallax. He calls it the one book whose literary structure he was able to compose deliberately, like a piece of music.

Caveat: He notes that his account converges with Antonio Negri's Empire, written from the opposite political direction, and does not treat the convergence as embarrassing. He also says the book's narrative stops at the American empire and that everything after it needed the later work — see the three projects of global domination.

He credits: Aristotle

The three projects of global domination Os três projetos de dominação global

The sequel to the Empire thesis, and the framework he is best known for outside philosophy: the forces now contesting world power are not one conspiracy but three separate projects of global domination — which he names provisionally the Russo-Chinese, the Western, and the Islamic — of comparable weight, sometimes allied and sometimes at each other's throats. He insists nobody can say which will prevail, including the people running them.

Where it comes from: He presents it as the continuation of O Jardim das Aflições, whose narrative stops at the American empire: the 2011 debate with Aleksandr Dugin is, in his words, the book's proper complement, carrying the analysis forward to the present.

He revised it: The naming shifts across the corpus. In 2009 he lists the communist project first and calls the third simply globalist; by 2013 the same three are 'the three globalisms'; and around 2020 he adds an observation rather than a correction, that the Western project is dismantling itself and the other two are collecting the winnings.

Caveat: He rejects the 'masters of the universe' reading his own audience often draws from this — the point of a three-way contest is that no one is in control. He also insists 'Anglo-American' is the wrong name for the Western project.

The triple intuition A tripla intuição

In any act of knowing, three things are grasped at once and cannot be separated: the act itself, the thing known, and the conditions that make the act possible. His model is sight — you never see an object without also seeing the light, and the light that shows the object is the same light that reaches the eye. He argues this connection is what the whole edifice of logic rests on, since a certainty available only in words could never underwrite a proof.

Where it comes from: He dates the idea to an argument with the position that there is no reality outside human language, and calls it, in the same breath, one of his great discoveries and one of the worst written things he ever produced — a draft that circulated as a course handout and was never made into a book.

Lineage: He built it to ground what he calls his radical intuitionism, and treats the near-universal symbolism of light and the sun as knowledge as evidence that the structure is not his invention but a description.

The revolutionary mentality A mentalidade revolucionária

Not a political position but a mental disorder with a structure, which he says has been running for four centuries: a mind that inverts the direction of time, treating a promised future as the cause of the present; inverts the subject-object relation, so the theory tells reality what it must be; and inverts moral responsibility, so the harm done in pursuit of the promise is charged to whoever resisted. The landings of philosophy A teoria dos patamares

Philosophy advances by landings rather than by schools: certain things, once seen, cannot be unseen, and reasoning below them is not disagreement but regression. Aristotle's distinction between form and matter is his standing example. The claim is normative, not historical — nobody has the right to argue beneath a landing. And a landing that is superseded is not thereby cancelled: climbing to a higher one, he says, in no way denies the value or the present importance of those below it, which he maintains even for a model whose founding premise he has just called plainly false.

Caveat: Asked in class for the list of landings, he agreed it would be worth having and did not produce one. No general inventory exists — but for one field it does: in the opening session of the 2002 course he names the three models of the history of philosophy, rejects each, and then enumerates what each nonetheless makes it impermissible to ignore. That is the doctrine applied to a whole domain rather than asserted, and it is the only such application in the corpus.

The subject of history A teoria do sujeito da história

History is only ever the history of agents that act, and most of the subjects historians write about cannot act. Brazil, he argues, has no history — it is the stage on which one happens, since a juridical-territorial unit has never done anything. The condition he says nobody had thought to state is continuity in time: an action exhausted inside one lifetime never enters history at all. What qualifies is a body that can train its successors to carry on a programme begun before they were born.

On the count: The list of kinds shifts between tellings. In 2009 he names the Catholic Church, Freemasonry, the Communist Party and dynastic families; in 2010 the great religions, initiatic societies, dynasties and the Communist Party; in 2011 he replaces the fourth with spiritual entities — God, angels and demons — and states there are no others. The four shown here are the human register, on which every telling agrees.

Where it comes from: He says he had to settle the question before he could ask whether historical knowledge explains anything at all about the divisions he was trying to describe, and calls the result an entirely new theory of history which he believes correct and irrefutable.

Lineage: Dynasties are the case he develops furthest, and the one that carries the most weight in his political writing: aristocratic or plebeian families conscious of the durability of their own interests, who take children from the cradle and raise them to continue a programme — the Bourbons and the Tudors, and, on his reading, the Rockefellers and the Rothschilds. What makes a dynasty an agent is fidelity to dead mentors. He then treats the Bilderberg circle as a consortium of such dynasties rather than as an organisation, which is how this doctrine does the work his politics asks of it.

Caveat: The fourth kind, in the fullest telling, is not human: he counts God, angels and demons as historical agents on the same criterion, since they too persist and act. Recorded as his claim. He also says himself that the theory has a problem — he never managed to apply it in a book that was entirely about history.

The collective imbecile O imbecil coletivo

His most cited coinage, and by his own account not an insult about intelligence. The collective imbecile is not made of imbeciles: it is normal and sometimes very able people who, out of professional solidarity or political convenience, adopt habits of language that stop them thinking, and who make one another stupid by doing it together.

Lineage: Two habits he singles out as the mechanism working. The first he calls by its old rhetorical name, argumentum ad ignorantiam, in the form Brazil gave it: what I have not heard of does not exist, said with an air of superiority — which he called, after a decade of newspaper polemic, the most frequently used argument he met. The second is an inversion of pedantry. Pedantry means parading knowledge you do not have; here, he says, having the knowledge is the pedantry, and asking an interlocutor to know the subject is treated as an attempt to humiliate him. What then stands in for the abandoned duties of intelligence is politeness: a cultivated debate comes to mean one in which everybody says foolish things courteously.

Caveat: He never calls it a theory, and it is not presented as one here: the book is a collection of examples from Brazilian intellectual life, gathered from the late 1980s onward. It is in this panel because he defines the mechanism explicitly and returns to it as a concept.

The juvenile imbecile O imbecil juvenil

An observation rather than a theory, and one his readers took up as a working category: the adolescent who believes he is rebelling is aiming at the weakest authority available — parents who love him and will do anything for him — while submitting completely to the strongest, the peers who will not. He puts the mechanism underneath it as a neurotic need for affection that produces group dependence, and says the political and moral language such a person uses is decoration over a much cruder motive.

On the count: It is not an age. He is explicit that juvenile imbeciles are sixty and seventy years old: the essay describes a condition people carry into old age, not a phase they leave.

Where it comes from: A short article — he says he compressed the whole thing into a page and a half — published on his own site and later placed first in O Mínimo que Você Precisa Saber para Não Ser um Idiota (2013). Existential contradiction A contradição existencial

The three constants of history As constantes da história

Three tendencies, he says, hold across the whole of the human record and have never been falsified: population always grows; separated cultures move toward one another until they intercommunicate completely; and power integrates upward, the higher power destroying the intermediate powers with the help of the population below it. Globalisation is not a fourth thing added to these. It is what happens when people with the means to act understand the first three, at which point a process that was merely happening becomes deliberate and planned — so that, he says, globalisation is in a sense the name of human history itself.

Where it comes from: He credits each constant to someone else and claims only the assembly: the first to Malthus, the second to the geographer Ellsworth Huntington in The Mainsprings of Civilization, the third to Bertrand de Jouvenel in Du Pouvoir. He is emphatic that the Huntington in question is not Samuel Huntington of the clash of civilisations, whom he takes to be a descendant.

Lineage: Jouvenel is the one he keeps returning to, and he treats Du Pouvoir as the standing answer to Benedetto Croce's history as the history of liberty: over the last four or five centuries state authority always comes out strengthened and the citizen below always weakened, and always on the pretext of democratising. Every levelling, on this reading, produces a worse hierarchy, because whoever distributes power is by that fact above everyone it is distributed to. The third constant is also where a second doctrine starts rather than ends: the growth of power is the premise from which he builds the difference of power as constitutive of the species, which is its own entry here. The credit for the growth of power belongs to this one.

Caveat: He is elsewhere severe about exactly this kind of search. Civilisation, he says, is a figure of speech and not a scientific concept, so quantifying over civilisations is measurement applied to metaphor; and he states flatly that he believes no total interpretation of the historical process is possible. The constants are therefore offered as observed regularities of the human record, not as a law that explains it — a distinction he keeps, and one that the entry keeps with him.

The three enemies of democracy Os inimigos da democracia

He argues that democracy has only ever been definable against something, and that the something has changed three times: against the old regime, once the constitution of power became everyone's problem; against modern totalitarianism, which made the free circulation of knowledge a requirement Jefferson could not have imagined; and now against pseudo-democracy, since totalitarianism is finished as an idea and survives only under other names. Three things then threaten it, and all three arrive wearing its own vocabulary.

Where it comes from: He names the third himself — 'what I call liberal economicism' — and describes it as an inverted version of Marxist economic determinism: the belief that liberalising the economy liberalises everything else automatically. His counter-example was China, which he said had been receiving a market economy for ten years while the government grew more repressive, and which the market economy was making rich enough to buy more weapons.

Lineage: The first enemy is his reading of Gramsci: hegemony is the manufacture of a consensus so complete that nobody remembers it was ever argued, and the expanded state is the party-state under a new name, so that transferring public administration to organised civil society realises the totalitarian idea while sounding like its opposite. For the second he uses Georges Bernanos — democracy is not the opposite of dictatorship but its cause — and illustrates it with cultural democracy, which first distributes the highest values to everyone and then lets the beneficiaries decide the lowest are the highest.

Caveat: He set out three and said he would leave the rest for another time, so the list is explicitly partial rather than closed. In the floor questions he added two more in passing: the medicalisation of society, and the idea that some international body should decide these matters — liberal doctrine, he said, was formed fighting the national state and finds itself defenceless against the world Leviathan now forming.

Culture does not follow development

Against the view that a country must first grow rich and then acquire a high culture, he holds that culture is the orientation of a life toward what is worth living for, and so is a condition of intelligence rather than one of its ornaments. Culture, on his etymology, means cultivation and cult together: something that improves, and something owed devotion. Both point past any state of affairs to what Plato called the supreme good. The Brazilian constitutional definition — whatever bears witness to a mode of existence — he places at the exact antipodes, because a mode of existence is a fact and not a value.

Where it comes from: He raises it against Paulo Arantes's Um Departamento Francês de Ultramar, which asks why fifty years of the philosophy faculty at USP produced no philosopher and answers that a level of development must be reached first. Carvalho's reply is historical: no great burst of philosophical creativity has ever followed an economic boom. Athens produced the Academy amid socioeconomic decay and runaway inflation; scholasticism's largest contributions came from Ireland and England when England was five centuries from being a power; German idealism arrived when Germany was not yet a country.

Lineage: He takes the supreme good from Plato and the psychological consequence from Viktor Frankl, whose camp observation — that those who came through were the ones with something to serve — he treats as evidence that the loss of a sense of existence lies under the neuroses. What replaces it he describes as a love of insignificance: Mário Ferreira dos Santos out of print while the theses accumulate on sambistas, and culture understood in three degraded senses at once — entertainment, commercial product, and party propaganda.

Caveat: He is talking about Brazil and says so, and the argument is made from a platform funded by the industrialists whose Forum it was — the study of that Forum as an institution notes its corporate backing in exactly this period. The historical examples are offered as counter-cases to a claimed dependence, not as a demonstration that poverty produces philosophy, which he does not claim.

The cycle of intervention

He describes state intervention in labour relations as a closed loop that has been running in Brazil since Vargas, each turn creating the condition for the next. Government intervenes to protect employees; the charges decapitalise firms; the firms come to government for rescue; the rescue is paid for with higher taxes; higher taxes raise prices and cut purchasing power; the shortfall requires new social programmes; the programmes require more tax. His point is not that any single step is wrong but that seventy years of it should have been enough to draw the conclusion, and that failing to draw a conclusion from your own experience is a defect of sight rather than of information.

Where it comes from: Stated at the Fórum da Liberdade in 2005, on a panel about the culture of work, against Oded Grajew — a founder of the World Social Forum, the Porto Alegre meetings this Forum was created to answer. Grajew had left before his turn, which Carvalho remarked on.

Lineage: He then argued that the debate itself was staged wrongly: the Brazilian argument imitates Conservatives against Labour inside a settled constitutional order, when the real question is which forces confront each other. On the liberal side he counted the industrialist Jorge Gerdau and a handful of Rio Grande do Sul businessmen; on the other, the FARC, the Chilean MIR, the Foro de São Paulo with what he put at eighty-four member organisations, the European Union, the United Nations and the Ford, Rockefeller and MacArthur foundations. It is the argument his later work on global projects is built from, five years before he made it abroad.

Caveat: The second half is a set of factual claims, several of which are checkable and one of which does not survive checking. Philosophy as a project A filosofia como projeto

Philosophy was not born finished — and he argues that everything else follows from saying so. What is not born finished cannot have appeared as a completed structure, only as a project or an ambition to be realised; and its founders must have known it was one, or philosophical discussion could never have taken the form it does, validating and invalidating doctrines piecemeal where a religion would call the same move heresy. From which he draws a single conclusion about method: there is only one scientifically valid way to tell the history of philosophy, as the succession of stages in realising an original project — and of the attempts to alter, impugn or replace it. He presents the three principles as apodictic rather than empirical, established before opening any history of philosophy rather than inferred from one.

Caveat: He claims the method satisfies the Hegelian demand for unity of movement without Hegel's error of imposing unity on events that have none — because where the facts yield only the unity of a problem, of a confusion or of an enigma, that is what gets recorded. Whether it escapes the objection is not something this project adjudicates.

Against 'a man of his time' O senso da eternidade

Calling someone a man of his time he judges a very great piece of nonsense — and says the person who says it believes he is doing history while doing the exact opposite of history. Nobody can be exclusively of his own time; if everyone were, there would be no history at all. His case is Brentano, a nineteenth-century philosopher whose own century must be inferred from indirect signs while Aristotle is everywhere in him: an influence two thousand years old outweighing a contemporary one. What makes history possible is instead a capacity he calls the sense of eternity — being able to put one's head outside one's own time and judge it whole, the same faculty by which a person judges his own life and says he took a wrong turn. Without it we are a drop of water in a river, unable to see where it is going.

Lineage: The term is older than the 2002 course and he credits it elsewhere: reading Hugh of St Victor in lecture 33, he takes 'the sense of eternity' from Michel Veber's commentary on the second degree of the lower Taoist hierarchy, and calls it the first degree a person has to reach — what lets the intelligence enter a real relation with the structure of reality, outside which there are only passing impressions and cultural constructions still more evanescent than those. In an undated later lecture he calls it his final message in the documentary made about him, and says O Jardim das Aflições illustrates it structurally. It recurs across the corpus rather than sitting in one session.

Caveat: Two things keep this from being a simple rejection of historical situatedness, and both are his. He notes that the phrase is never used of Stalin or Hitler, because calling them men of their time would mean conceding that we produced them, and nobody wants that responsibility. And he demands the opposite of indifference to one's own moment: your whole intellectual and personal life, he says, has to unfold inside an alert consciousness of the real situation you are living in — you have to know where and when you are living. What he rejects is not situatedness but its counterfeit, being the man of the day, the week or the month with a view of the world bounded by whatever is being talked about.

The difference of power A fenomenologia do poder / a diferença de poder

That the immeasurable difference of power between members of the species is not a political arrangement but a constitutive element of the species itself. He reaches it by joining two authors: Jouvenel's finding in Du Pouvoir that power always grows, and Ibn Khaldun on prophetic authority, which he says three times is what put him on the line of enquiry. He then reports going to look for the same thing in animals, and concludes from the contrast that the maximum of violence one human being does to another is always statal — no criminal, gang or murderer kills as many as a government — so that the juridical ordering of society tracks the increase in killing, modern democracies included.

On the count: Lectures 79 and 115 both carry unresolved date disagreements in the corpus — 79's header reads 2012 against an index, filenames and a surrounding sequence that all support 2010 — so both are entered unverified. The chain runs end to end only in lecture 70; the zoology, the licence to kill and the statal conclusion appear in no other file.

Where it comes from: He tells it as a moment of discovery, and calls it o estalo — the click. Reading Jouvenel on the growth of power alongside Ibn Khaldun on prophetic authority, the two struck together and gave him the constitutive claim. He arrives at the same conclusion by a second and independent route elsewhere, through Raskolnikov rather than through Jouvenel, and there names it as his own: one of the themes of his political philosophy, the theme of the difference of power.

Caveat: Two things this entry records as his and does not adjudicate. The first is a factual claim about animals: that zoologists know of only some twelve hundred species in which a member kills another of the same species, that gorillas settle by display and wolves by the position of the tail, and that homo homini lupus therefore libels the wolf. The second is a qualification he attaches to the conclusion and which is easy to drop: the difference between democracy and dictatorship on this measure he calls very relative and quantitative — real, but not deep enough to mark a difference of essence.

Parallel storylines

The chronology read as parallel storylines: one row per lane, one column per decade, each cell the number of that lane’s events in that decade. An event that belongs to several storylines is counted in each, so the rows sum to more than the number of events. Select a number to jump to that decade in the chronology.

These lanes are an editorial reading of a contested life, not a neutral fact: the currents are the ones he himself named as his formation — in a 2012 lecture he called it his great sin to have spent 'thirty-some years absorbing all the influences' (per the community transcriptions) — plus the public roles the record documents. Lanes are containers for dated events, never a claim that the currents weigh the same. Left out as lanes, deliberately: family life (private; it appears only where already public), the transcription corpus's internal scholarship (its own project), and the Brazilian New Right ecosystem around him (a distinct, living-persons subject).

Disambiguation & nuance

Almost everything about this subject is contested terrain — his title, his influence, his death, and his own retellings of his trajectory. This section records the distinctions the sources themselves make.

olavodecarvalho.org is no longer his site

As of August 2026 the live domain returns a page of unrelated e-commerce spam (apparently lapsed and squatted). His actual site must be cited via Internet Archive snapshots (e.g. the 15 December 2021 capture). Do not link the live domain as an official site.[34]

'Philosopher' is a claimed and contested title

He styled himself a philosopher and taught philosophy for decades, but held no degree in the field (he left PUC-Rio's Conpefil without completing studies); mainstream obituaries write 'self-described philosopher', his students insist on the title without qualification. This site records who calls him what.[41] [4]

Two works named O Jardim das Aflições

The 1995 book (Diadorim) and the 2017 documentary about him (dir. Josias Teófilo) share the name; AdoroCinema additionally mislabels the film as 2015.[42]

Not the other Carvalhos

Not the historian José Murilo de Carvalho (d. 2023), the poet Ronald de Carvalho, or the politician Salatiel Carvalho — search results routinely conflate them.[40]

'Guru of Bolsonaro' is an attributed label

The international press (NYT: 'Bolsonaro's Far-Right Guru') and much of the Brazilian press used the label; Olavo publicly rejected it, and the scholar Mark Sedgwick considers it overstated. It appears on this site only as someone's characterization, never in the site's own voice.[8] [30]

The COF corpus is cited, not reproduced

The Curso Online de Filosofia's community transcriptions (585 lectures) are public and of varying review quality, and the lecture header dates carry known defects. This project cites lectures by aula number and date only; the reception index in data/cof-reception.json records which lectures engage which philosopher.[37] [38]

The 2009 course is not where the teaching began

The Curso Online de Filosofia is routinely treated as the start of his teaching, and the two names are often used for one thing. The record separates them. He was teaching courses in Brazil for more than twenty years before 2008, by his own count; the Seminário de Filosofia was meeting in person in 2002, in the same room as the recorded history-of-philosophy course and on the following day; after the 2005 emigration it reached São Paulo by videoconference from the United States, and in April 2007 he announced a session for the 18th of that month. seminariodefilosofia.org opened in August 2008 to publish the back catalogue, and the COF's first class followed on 14 March 2009 after a five-month slip. In August 2009 he still lists the Seminário and the COF as separate calls on his week. So 2009 is when the teaching was industrialised, not when it started — and the Seminário's own founding date remains unverified.[76] [77] [36]
