Frithjof Schuon (1907–1998)

Swiss-German writer and metaphysician, the leading exponent of the perennialist (Traditionalist) school in the twentieth century alongside René Guénon and Ananda Coomaraswamy. Initiated into Sufism in Algeria in 1933, he directed from Europe — and, after 1980, from Bloomington, Indiana — the order that became known as the Maryamiyya, and wrote dozens of books on metaphysics, comparative religion and sacred art. The accounts of his life here follow chiefly his estate's site and the historian of Traditionalism Mark Sedgwick.[3] [4] [5]

Vida y obra

AñoAcontecimiento
18 June 1907Born in Basel, Switzerland, son of a German violinist.[3]
1920After his father's death, moves with his mother to Mulhouse, in French Alsace.[3]
1930Settles in Paris, where he works and deepens his study of Arabic and of Eastern doctrines.[3]
1933Travels to Mostaganem, Algeria, enters Islam and spends about four months at the zawiya of the Sufi shaykh Ahmad al-Alawi, from whom he receives initiation (the date per the estate's site; other reconstructions place the departure from Europe in late 1932).[3]
1935Publishes his first book, in German: Leitgedanken zur Urbesinnung (Primordial Meditation).[3]
1941Settles in Lausanne, Switzerland, his base for nearly four decades.[3]
1948Publishes De l'Unité transcendante des religions, his first and best-known book in French, the classic formulation of the perennialist thesis.[3]
1949Marries Catherine Feer; obtains Swiss citizenship shortly afterwards.[3]
1959 and 1963Travels to the American West at the invitation of Indigenous friends; on the 1959 visit he is adopted by the Sioux (and, in 1984, by the Crow).[3]
1970s ≈Publishes mature works such as Logic and Transcendence, Form and Substance in the Religions and Esoterism as Principle and as Way.[3]
1980Emigrates with his wife to the United States, settling in Bloomington, Indiana, near a community of disciples; in the first years there publishes From the Divine to the Human, To Have a Center and Roots of the Human Condition.[3]
1991Becomes the target, in Bloomington, of accusations taken to a grand jury on the complaints of a former disciple; the prosecution dropped the charges shortly afterwards — an episode documented by the historian Mark Sedgwick in Against the Modern World (2004) and discussed on his academic blog.[5] [4]
5 May 1998Dies in Bloomington, Indiana.[3]
