Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716)

German polymath — philosopher, mathematician, jurist and diplomat — counted among the great rationalists of the seventeenth century. He developed the differential and integral calculus independently of Newton and formulated a metaphysical system centered on the monads and pre-established harmony. He spent most of his career in the service of the House of Hanover.[3] [4] [5]

Vida y obra

AñoAcontecimiento
1 July 1646Born in Leipzig (21 June by the Julian calendar then in use; 1 July by the Gregorian, as Britannica records).[4]
1661Enters the University of Leipzig, beginning his formation in philosophy and law.[3]
1667Completes his doctorate in law; the same year he meets Baron von Boineburg, who secures him a position at the court of the Elector of Mainz.[3]
1672–1676Diplomatic mission in Paris, then the center of European science, with a brief journey to London (1673); he frequents the leading savants of the age.[3] [4]
late 1675Lays the foundations of the differential and integral calculus, per Britannica.[4]
1676Enters the service of the House of Hanover as librarian to Duke Johann Friedrich; on the way, meets Spinoza in Amsterdam (18–21 November).[3]
1686Writes the Discourse on Metaphysics and begins the correspondence with Arnauld, central expositions of his mature metaphysics.[3]
1710Publishes the Essays of Theodicy, the only philosophical book he published in his lifetime.[3]
1714Writes the Monadology and the Principles of Nature and Grace, final syntheses of his system; that year his patron Georg Ludwig becomes George I of England, but Leibniz remains in Hanover.[3]
14 November 1716Dies in Hanover.[3] [4]
